The Kid I Used To Be

July 10, 2026

Somebody asked me last month, kneeling on a client's living room floor running speaker cable through a wall, what I actually wanted to do with my life. Not meanly. Just the small talk people make while you're on your knees with a drill and they're sipping coffee they didn't have to walk anywhere for. I gave him the usual answer, something about the trade being steady, good money, nothing to complain about. All true. None of it the real answer. The real one is a conversation I've been having with myself since I was nineteen, and driving home that night it kept circling back to him, the kid I used to be, the one who was certain he was going to do something that mattered and would probably be a little disappointed watching me hand a stranger a version of my life that fit inside a coffee break.

That's the first thing I'd tell him. Not that the trade is beneath anything, it isn't, I'm good at it and I like it fine. But that somewhere between nineteen and now I started calling my fear "being practical" and never once called it by its real name. Every safe decision got dressed up as maturity. Every risk I didn't take got filed under responsible. Most of what passes for practicality in a life is just fear wearing a tie to work. You don't feel it happening in real time. It happens the way rust happens, slow enough that by the time you notice, you've started mistaking it for the shape the metal was always going to take.

He also had a lot of opinions back then, and he held every one of them like a piece of his own body, like giving one up meant giving up an arm. I don't know exactly when I learned that the opinions worth having are the ones you're willing to take out back every so often and hit with a stick, just to see if they're still standing on their own or only standing because you're too proud to let them fall. Most people pick a side in their twenties and spend the rest of their life defending the flag instead of asking whether the country underneath it still makes any sense. I've had to bury beliefs I was once ready to die on. It didn't feel like losing an argument. It felt like finally setting down something heavy I'd been carrying without noticing the weight, the way your shoulders drop after you take off a backpack you forgot you were still wearing.

I'd also want to know why he spent so much of his twenties telling people what he couldn't stand instead of what he loved. Easier, I guess, to build a whole identity out of the things you're against. You get to sound sharp without ever being exposed. I did this with music, with movies, with entire countries, mine included. It took embarrassingly long to notice that the people I actually admired never talked that way. They just talked about what they loved until you loved it too, whether it was an old Ahmad Zahir record or a rug or an amplifier built the year my father was born. Contempt is cheap. Love costs you something, because you have to admit it matters to you, and somebody can use that against you if they want to. Damn, that one still gets me when I write it down. I'd tell him to spend the money anyway.

And I'd tell him, gently, because some part of him already suspected this and it scared him half to death: none of it is being tracked. There is no cosmic ledger keeping score for a repairman from a country most people can't find on a map, running cable through some rich guy's wall on a Tuesday. That should be terrifying, and somehow, once you actually sit with it, it isn't. If nothing out there is handing you a reason, you get to build one, out of whatever you actually want, not whatever looks defensible written down on paper. My father crossed a border with almost nothing so that one day I'd get to choose what to do with an afternoon. I don't think he did that so I could spend the afternoon being careful.

I never gave that guy in the living room the real answer. I gave it, fifteen years too late, to a nineteen year old instead, in a truck, alone, radio off so I could hear myself actually say it out loud. That's the only commencement speech I've got in me, and the only graduate in the audience is a version of myself who needed to hear all of it and can't anymore.
