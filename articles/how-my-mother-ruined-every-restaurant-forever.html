How My Mother Ruined Every Restaurant Forever

Published September 10, 2017

There's one dish that exists only in my mother's kitchen.

Not in restaurants. Not on YouTube. Only in that kitchen, in one specific pot that should have been thrown out in 2009 and has somehow outlasted every appliance around it.

I'll spend the rest of my life trying, and failing, to make it myself in a country that's never heard of it. I once stood in a grocery store at twenty-nine holding a bunch of cilantro like it had personally betrayed my bloodline.

For me it's Shorwa.

Not the fancy kind. Not the restaurant version with three decorative herbs placed like evidence at a crime scene. Real Shorwa. A bowl with no branding and no performance. Soup. Bread. Radishes. Green onions. And the emotional stability of my entire family history.

Every family has a dish like this. Maybe it's your mother's qabuli. Your aunt's mantu. Aush that could legally qualify as medicine. Or something so specific the only way to describe it is: "You know... the yellow one."

The tragedy is that my mother never gave me the recipe. Not because she's hiding it. She just doesn't have one.

I asked her once. "How much salt?"

"Depends on the person. And the size of the meal."

"So how much is that?"

"Enough."

I wanted teaspoons. I wanted the kind of precision that prevents emotional damage. What I got was a woman who tastes, adjusts, and refuses to elaborate. I follow recipes exactly and still make food that tastes like an apology.

I've watched her cook the way people watch surgeons. Silent, respectful, slightly afraid. She taps the pot lid once. She smells the air. "It needs five more minutes." I've never once found out how she knows.

Grocery shopping with her is a military operation. She doesn't trust American vegetables. She squeezes tomatoes like she's interrogating them and puts cucumbers back with real disappointment. "No flavor." She'll drive forty minutes past seven normal grocery stores to get to one tiny Afghan market wedged between a laundromat and a tax office because, in her words, the onions there understand her.

She's right, for the record. I learned that in September 2017.

All the cousins had flown out to surprise one of our cousins for her birthday while she was away at school. It was one of those chaotic family trips where nobody sleeps, everyone argues about directions, and twelve people end up sharing one charger and one opinion.

On the way back, my mother called. Not to ask if I'd landed safely. Not to ask if I was tired. She had a mission.

"Pick up Afghan bread."

Not one bread. An absurd amount of bread. Enough to suggest we were either catering a wedding or preparing for the end of civilization.

So I stopped at the classic, slightly suspicious Afghan store, the one with the flickering bulb, people yelling across the room, and the general energy of a flea market crossed with a family argument. Objectively chaos. Spiritually home.

While I waited for the bread I drifted over to the vegetables. Fresh herbs. Radishes. Green onions. And I was somewhere else entirely. Back home, back in the gardens we used to tend for months, picking vegetables with our own hands, in a life where a plain bowl of Shorwa felt like luxury because gratitude was one of the ingredients.

The owner caught me staring and asked if I wanted something. I told him no, I was just remembering. Missing the old days. Missing meals where people were grateful for simple things.

Maybe he heard it in my voice. Without another word he took my hand and pulled me to the side, where two older men were cutting radishes and green onions for fresh Shorwa. I told him I didn't want to intrude or leave anyone hungry. His exact words: "Then we all eat less, but we eat together. You're our guest, and it was your destiny to join us in this humble meal."

Then one of them smashed an onion against the ground with his fist instead of using a knife like a normal citizen.

Instantly I was eight years old again, back in Kabul. Dusty streets. Street carts. Food made by a man with a crust of dirt under his fingernails, while every passing car threw up enough dust and smoke and mystery particles to count as seasoning.

And it was perfect.

Yes, there was a real chance I'd regret it later. Spiritually euphoric, physically unpredictable. Worth it anyway.

I sat down. The four of us ate that Shorwa like it was sacred. No plating, no fusion, no "deconstructed comfort food." Soup, bread, radishes, green onions, and the kind of silence that only happens when the food is doing all the talking.

It's still the best meal I've had outside my mother's kitchen.

The best food is never just food. It's biography. It tells you who survived what, who cooked with plenty, who cooked with nothing, who made miracles out of leftovers. My parents' generation can look at half an onion and three potatoes and make something that tastes like gratitude. I look at the same ingredients and order takeout.

Nobody in my family says "I know life has been hard." They say "Eat first." Nobody says "I missed you." They say "I made your favorite." Which is worse, honestly, because now I'm crying over soup.

I moved out eventually. Started saying things like "I cook for myself now." Very grown. Then I'd visit, my mother would ask what I wanted to eat, and I'd be six years old again. Everything. I want everything. The soup, the bread, the rice, the thing that takes fourteen hours and mild divine intervention.

Maybe the recipe was never supposed to be exact, because memory isn't exact either. You burn the first batch, call home, try again, and learn your version will never taste exactly like hers. It isn't supposed to. It's mine now. A little different. A little translated. Still home.

There's an old saying: naan o piyaaz, peshaani waaz. Bread and onions, forehead held high. Sometimes dignity doesn't come from luxury. Sometimes it comes from a plastic chair in a questionable Afghan store, eating hot Shorwa with strangers who felt like family within twenty minutes.

And realizing, for one perfect moment, I was never homesick for a place.

I was homesick for being known.
