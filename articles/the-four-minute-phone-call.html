The Four-Minute Phone Call

Published June 1, 2022

When I lived on my own in San Diego for school at SDSU, my parents called every week.

The call lasted somewhere between four and seven minutes. Emotionally, it carried thirty years, three countries, two unfinished arguments, and at least one sacrifice nobody was allowed to mention directly.

It always started the same way.

"Hello?"
"Yes, hello."
"Can you hear me?"
"Yes."
"Why are you sounding far?"
"I'm not far."
"You are sounding far."

Then the questions, in the same order every week.

"Did you eat?"
Yes.
"What did you eat?"
Food.
"Real food or nonsense?"

And we're under attack before the first minute is up.

"Did you eat?" was never about food. It meant: are you taking care of yourself, are you surviving out there, do you still remember where you come from, are you secretly living on iced coffee and bad decisions.

Then came the financial review.

"Are you saving money?"
Not really.
"Don't spend too much."
Thank you. I hadn't considered simply being rich.

I'd mention a coffee, and suddenly I'd dishonored three generations. "Seven dollars? We have coffee at home." Yes, but the coffee at home tastes like responsibility.

Sometimes they'd ask about marriage. Casually, the way a sniper is casual.

"So... your friend got engaged."

Then work. "How is your job?" Fine. "Just fine?" Yes, because if I tell you the truth we'll both need therapy, and neither of us believes in it.

Everything that mattered on those calls lived in what didn't get said. My mother saying "you sound tired" meant she'd detected spiritual exhaustion through Bluetooth. My father asking "everything okay?" once, very quietly, meant he'd been worried for three weeks.

But nobody said "I miss you." Nobody said "I'm proud of you." Nobody said "I was scared when we moved here and I still don't know if I did it right."

Instead they said: "Wear a jacket."

Which, translated properly, means: I would fight God himself if it kept you warm.

People from outside hear a four-minute call and assume distance. It's the opposite. The love is practical. It comes in the form of logistics, and it's criticism disguised as concern disguised as criticism again. It's deeply confusing and very effective.

And then the guilt. They don't even have to say it. One pause. One sigh. One "It's okay, don't worry about us."

That sentence has flattened better men than me. Suddenly I remember everything. My father starting over in a country that didn't care what he'd been back home. The language they had to learn at an age when nobody learns a language. The way they made themselves smaller so my life could be bigger.

And now they're asking if I ate lunch, and I'm saying yeah, standing in my kitchen staring at a frozen burrito like it personally betrayed my ancestors.

Sometimes after we hung up I'd just sit there for a second looking at the screen, thinking how strange it is that the people who would give me their organs still couldn't say "I'm proud of you" without sounding like they were filing taxes.

I'm the same, honestly. Maybe that's inherited too. Maybe the real family heirloom isn't jewelry or recipes. It's a generational inability to say "I miss you" without bringing fruit.

But the calls have started changing. My parents repeat stories now. They ask the same question twice. They say "when are you visiting?" in a voice that sounds lighter than it is.

These calls aren't permanent. One day there will be a last one, and I won't know it when it happens. It'll just be another Tuesday. Another "Did you eat?" Another "Yes, I ate." Another "Okay, good."

For years I treated those calls like chores. Something to return later, rush through, answer while half-reading emails. One day I'd trade almost anything for one more boring four-minute call about soup. One more lecture about money. One more passive-aggressive weather warning.

So I stay on longer now. I ask more questions. I let the silence sit instead of trying to get out of it. I say "I love you" first, even when everyone reacts like I just joined a cult.

My parents may never say it the way the movies taught me love should sound. They've been saying it my whole life anyway. In bills paid. In sacrifices nobody mentions. In plane tickets they never bought for themselves. In every annoying, repetitive call.

"Did you eat?"

Yes.

I know what you mean.

And yes. I love you too.
