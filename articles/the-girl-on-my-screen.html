The Girl on My Screen

Published November 2, 2025

I didn't mean to find her.

Nobody means to find anything at that hour. Thumb up, thumb up, thumb up. It's less browsing than controlled falling. Most nights it's the same recycled content in different faces. Then, every once in a while, something stops you cold.

That night it was a girl I recognized.

Not her, exactly. I recognized the family. I'd known her older sibling. I knew the household and the kind of home she came from. Conservative. Traditional. The kind of family where appearances aren't just kept up, they're inherited, ironed into you from birth. Shoes by the door. A grandmother's eyes in the back of every room.

And here was this girl, dancing for a camera, for strangers, for an audience that would move on in four seconds no matter what she did.

I kept scrolling. Then I stopped. Then I went back.

Not for the reason you might think. I went back because I couldn't shake the image of her mother.

I don't know her mother personally, but I know enough to picture her. A woman from that world sits down with a cup of tea she's been reheating for three hours, opens her phone with the care of someone who has never fully trusted it, and finds her daughter doing exactly this.

I tried to imagine what hits first. Probably rage, eventually. But before the rage, I think it's something without a clean name. The feeling of watching everything you gave up to raise a child show up on a screen in a shape you never pictured. You argued about her curfew. You bought her first laptop. You did not picture this.

My brain, doing what brains do when they're left unsupervised at night, pulled out the Hitler question. The philosophers' favorite party trick: if you could go back and kill Hitler as a baby, would you?

Bear with me.

The version I kept thinking about was quieter and worse. If that mother had known how this chapter would go, would she have made different choices? Would she have had children at all?

I sat with that longer than was probably healthy, and landed here: it's the wrong question, and we keep asking it anyway.

No child arrives already performing for strangers. Nobody is born hungry for the wrong kind of attention, already running the math on likes and views before she knows who she is. Every girl on every screen was once a kid in somebody's house, being shaped slowly and constantly by everything around her. By what was said and what never was. By what got rewarded and what got quietly tolerated. By how the adults around her talked about women when they thought nobody was listening. And by us, the people who stop scrolling long enough to watch.

You can't kill the Hitler baby, because the Hitler baby isn't Hitler yet. And her mother couldn't have seen this coming, because the girl in that video isn't the product of one household. She's the product of everything we've all agreed to live inside.

What made me put my phone down was the app. It didn't create her. It found her. It found exactly what she was missing, attention and a number going up, and handed her a stage. The audience on the other side doesn't care about her, won't remember her name by the next video, and will tune out the second she stops performing.

And I was in that audience. I gave her the view. The machine counted it the same as everyone else's, whatever I was feeling while I watched.

I doomscrolled into something I didn't want to find, and the discomfort turned out not to be about her. It was about how easily I'd become part of the crowd she was performing for.

The scroll doesn't stop for anyone. That night, for a minute, I stopped it anyway.
