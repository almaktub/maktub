Eight Years

July 24, 2026

Somebody asked me last week why I still bring up the Beatles like it's 1964, and by the time I got to my second sentence I could already see the fifteen-second timer running out behind their eyes. That's not on them specifically. That's everyone now. We've all got the attention span of a goldfish, rating four decades of a person's life's work on whether the first eight seconds slap. If the hook doesn't grab you before you finish your coffee, you're gone, scrolling to the next thing, and God forbid a song ask you to sit with it. I gave up trying to write anything that resembles literary criticism a long time ago. This is just a brain dump. I'm putting it down anyway, whether anybody sticks around to read it or not.

Because I do actually believe it, not as a bit, not as bait for a group chat argument: the Beatles are the greatest band that has ever fucking existed, and it isn't close. Start with the part nobody ever puts in the highlight reel.

Before any of the mop-top screaming and the Ed Sullivan lights, there were two years in Hamburg, playing clubs on the Reeperbahn, the city's red-light district, to sailors and gangsters and whoever else wandered in off the street looking for something to do at two in the morning. The club owner's whole directive was "Mach Schau," make a show, keep playing no matter what, and they did. Eight-hour sets some nights, longer on weekends, six nights a week, for kids barely out of their teens. Name me one band today willing to do that. Not for a festival slot, not for a clip that might do numbers. Eight hours a night, in a district built for sailors blowing off steam, learning how to hold a room that had no reason to give them one.

That's why I don't think the stadiums ever meant what everyone assumes they meant. By the time Shea Stadium sold out in 1965, fifty-five thousand people screaming so loud the band couldn't hear their own amps over the crowd, they'd already logged more hours playing live than most touring acts rack up in a full career. The stadium wasn't the dream. It was just Hamburg, but louder. They'd already proven the only thing that actually needed proving, that they could play, night after night, in a room that didn't care who they were yet. A year later they walked away from touring completely, Candlestick Park, 1966, last show, because standing in front of a crowd that couldn't hear them and that they couldn't hear back had stopped being the point. The point had already happened, years earlier, in a smoky room above a strip club, for an audience that would've booed them off if they weren't actually good.

Then there's the chemistry, its own miracle stacked on top of that work ethic. Four guys who fought, who had every reason on paper to hate each other by the end, who get blamed for the breakup in a dozen different directions depending on who's telling the story that day (ask three people and you'll get three different culprits, Yoko Ono usually gets nominated first, fairly or not) still managed to sound, on tape, like four instruments built for exactly this. That kind of chemistry isn't something you coach into a band. You either have it or you're faking it, and everyone can hear the difference eventually.

Take them one at a time and it gets funnier, and sadder. John was the one who'd tell you the truth about yourself whether you asked for it or not, on record and off, writing like a man who'd already decided honesty was going to cost him something so he might as well get paid for it. Paul was the one who could write you a funeral hymn and a beach anthem in the same afternoon and make both sound effortless, genuinely too talented to commit to one genre, so he never did. George got filed under "the quiet one" for so long that people forgot he was writing some of the best songs on the back half of the catalog while nobody was looking, and he went out and found God in a sitar before most of the West even knew there was a God to find that way. Ringo gets a joke made about him more than any drummer in history, that he wasn't even the best drummer in his own band, and it still isn't true. The man held four enormous personalities together with a backbeat and a sense of humor, which might be the hardest instrument anybody in that room was playing.

Then there's the math nobody actually runs. People talk about the Beatles like they were a permanent fixture, some institution that had been putting out records for half a century. They weren't. Their entire recording career runs about eight years, start to finish. Eight years, twelve albums, almost no duds in the stack. Most bands need eight years just to figure out who they are. The Beatles used theirs to invent, discard, and reinvent an entire genre four separate times, then walked away at the top, bored, because there was nobody left in the building who could catch them.

I think about that part more than the songs sometimes. What that must have felt like, to look around at everyone else making music and realize nobody was gaining ground on you. That's not a flex. That's lonely. I'd bet good money that's a real piece of why they split, on top of everything else that's been written about it. It's hard to stay hungry when you've already eaten the whole table.

I spend most of my week on my knees in somebody else's wall, running speaker cable, so I notice the technical side of this more than most people would. The Beatles started recording in mono, the tambourine-horn-out-of-a-turntable era, before stereo was standard in most living rooms. Then stereo became the format and they remixed for two speakers instead of one. Then the format changed again and their whole catalog got remastered for CD. Most artists get handed one format and one set of tools for an entire career. The Beatles got dragged through three, in eight years, and sounded essential in every one of them. I've stood in front of a client's turntable more than once explaining why a mono pressing sounds different from the stereo remaster sitting right next to it, and every time, somewhere in the middle of that explanation, I end up back at the same fact: this band adapted to formats that didn't exist yet when they started.

Genre-wise, good luck boxing them in. Somebody tries to tell me they're a pop band, I point them at "Helter Skelter," a song people genuinely credit as one of the ancestors of heavy metal, written years before metal had a name. Then I keep going, because the list doesn't stop there. They opened a single with guitar feedback on purpose ("I Feel Fine," 1964), back when feedback was still something you apologized for. They put a sitar on a pop record before most of America could've pointed to India on a map ("Norwegian Wood," 1965). In April of 1964 they held all top five spots on the Billboard Hot 100 at the same time, a record nobody has come within shouting distance of since. They treated an album as one continuous piece of art instead of a pile of singles with filler between them (Sgt. Pepper's, 1967, still gets cited as the reason concept albums exist at all). They shot actual short films to promote songs like "Strawberry Fields Forever" and "Penny Lane" in 1967, which is just a music video with a different name, about fifteen years before MTV existed to put one on it. And their engineer at Abbey Road invented double tracking because John Lennon got tired of singing the same line twice. That one studio trick, born out of Lennon's laziness, is sitting underneath half of modern music right now, Tame Impala included, whether the artist making it knows where it came from or not.

And they left the mistakes in. The count-ins, the studio chatter, the takes where somebody's voice cracks. Most artists spend a fortune scrubbing every imperfection out of a record until it sounds like it was assembled by a machine. The Beatles let you hear four guys in a room, actually being in the room. That's the whole appeal, and it drives me insane that it's the first thing people scroll past.

And somewhere close to half of your favorite artists only picked up an instrument in the first place because of them. Bruce Springsteen has said it outright. So has Billy Joel. So has Tom Petty. Oasis spent their entire career trying to be them and barely bothered hiding it, Noel Gallagher's called Rubber Soul his favorite record more times than anybody's counted, and half the people singing along to "Wonderwall" at a wedding right now have no idea they're singing along to a Beatles tribute band that got famous enough to stop being a tribute band. Jeff Lynne built ELO on the explicit idea of picking up where "I Am the Walrus" left off, and did it well enough that decades later the surviving Beatles brought him in to produce their own unfinished songs. Seventy-three million people watched them on Ed Sullivan in February of 1964, still one of the most-watched broadcasts in American television history, and an entire generation of kids went out and bought a guitar the next morning because four guys from Liverpool made it look like something worth doing with your life. You're not just listening to whatever's actually your favorite band. In some direct or indirect way, you're listening to a band that exists because the Beatles got there first.

So here's my actual complaint, since I already told you I was dumping this and not polishing it into something respectable: give something more than fifteen seconds before you decide it isn't for you. Not just the Beatles. A book, a person, a whole genre you already made your mind up about in high school. Nobody wants to put in eight hours in a smoky room anymore either, not as a listener and not as an artist, and maybe that's the actual thread running under all of this. The good stuff rarely leads with the hook. Sometimes it makes you wait for it, on purpose, because it knows it's going to be worth it once it lands.

Sixty-six years after four kids from Liverpool started a band with no business becoming the best one in the world, you could walk into a village with no power grid and somebody there could still hum you a Beatles song. For a while they were the most famous people on earth next to Jesus Christ, until Michael Jackson came along and took the title. That's its own argument for another day.
