Where Was This Policy?

Published October 30, 2026

I was at my desk untangling a nest of cables when B-dawg called. Officially, she was calling because I'd been on a random date and owed her the full report. Unofficially, it was us, so twenty minutes later we were nowhere near the date.

B-dawg is my cousin B. She has three kids, and at any given hour all three are climbing on her, or all three need her attention at the exact same second, or they're just being kids at full volume. She'd raised her voice at them, and her mother, my aunt, was in the room. My aunt told her to calm down. Be gentler with them. Be more patient.

"Calm down" has a remarkable success rate for two words that have never calmed anyone down.

She wasn't just venting. She knows she loses her patience sometimes, and she's been trying to catch it. And I, having been invited into a conversation about patience, immediately ran out of it on her behalf.

I told her she was allowed to be overwhelmed. Having kids doesn't mean swallowing every feeling until something gives. If everyone in that house gets to be loud except the one person keeping them all alive, she isn't the problem. And by my very generous definition, raising your voice once in a while still counts as gentle parenting. There are probably books that disagree with me. I have thoughtfully avoided reading them.

I meant every word. I still do.

But she'd told me two other things, and I talked straight past both. This wasn't a one-time comment. My aunt had been watching it happen for a while. And my aunt had said something else: that she felt she'd never made her own daughter feel safe enough to come to her.

I didn't hear it. I was busy building the case, and I had the closing argument ready, the one everybody reaches for when a parent hands out advice they never took themselves. Where was this policy when you were raising us?

It's a fair question. A parent's regret doesn't give you a different childhood.

But I know a few things about unsolicited advice from older relatives, mostly from ignoring it. A while back an uncle watched me fight a problem at work. He could see exactly where I was going wrong, so he told me, without being asked. I was too locked in to hear it. I nodded and kept doing it my own stupid way. It took me much longer than it should have to understand he wasn't showing off. He'd already made that mistake. He was trying to save me the trip.

My aunt watched her daughter raise her voice at her kids and recognized it, because she'd done it herself, and the person she'd done it to was standing right there. She could have kept quiet and spared herself the obvious comeback. She said something anyway, clumsily, opening with the two worst words available.

That isn't a lecture. That's a mother admitting to her own kid that she got it wrong, and trying to keep it from happening again.

B-dawg needed someone to hear how hard she was trying. My aunt needed someone to hear how hard it is to look back. I was so ready to take one side that I almost missed they were asking for the same thing.

I didn't listen to my uncle either.

I'm only now getting around to it.
