Not This One

Published March 16, 2026

There's a very specific pause that happens when you tell someone you're not on the dating apps.

Not a long pause. Just a beat. You can watch them doing math.

"Have you tried Hinge?"

I have.

"Bumble?"

Also yes.

"Okay, but what about Muzz? It's specifically for..."

I know what it is.

The pause again. Then, gently, like they're breaking news to someone who doesn't know he's lost: "So... why not?"

The honest answer sounds paranoid out loud and reasonable only inside my head. Here it is anyway. The algorithm already has everything else.

Spotify knew I was in a depressed era before I did. I was driving somewhere, feeling fine, and the playlist quietly drifted toward Sufjan Stevens. A warning shot. I checked in with myself. Oh. Yes. Something is off. The machine diagnosed it first.

Netflix kept me up last month because it understood, before I'd consciously decided anything, that I wasn't really trying to sleep. I was avoiding tomorrow. So it queued the next episode. Helpful and evil at the same time.

Google Maps rerouted me last week without asking. Somewhere I've driven a hundred times. It suggested a different road and I took it like a loyal citizen. Got there fine. Didn't think twice until I pulled up.

The algorithm knows what I think is funny, what I should be angry about, and what fashion era I've apparently entered without noticing. It has my mood, my sleep, my Tuesday afternoon. It knows things about me I haven't said out loud to anyone.

And now someone wants me to hand it my love life too.

I get the appeal. Dating is confusing, and apps make it faster, which people mistake for easier. Instead of spending four months on the wrong person before finding the fatal incompatibility, you find it in forty minutes over mediocre coffee. Efficient. Honest. Good engineering.

That's the word that stops me. Engineering.

I think, and I'm aware this is the kind of thing people who die alone say, that the irrational part is the point. The wrong-time, wrong-city, wrong-everything person you end up with anyway. The conversation that was supposed to take twenty minutes and took four hours. Whatever makes absolutely no algorithmic sense.

An algorithm would have told Romeo: "Based on your profile and stated preferences, Juliet is an 83% compatibility match. However, family conflict indicators suggest a 12% long-term viability score. Would you like to see other profiles in the Verona area?"

The algorithm doesn't know timing. It doesn't know the look someone gives you when they're also trying to leave a party early. It knows patterns, and most things that have ever mattered to me showed up sideways, with no profile and nobody's best angles picked out in advance. They arrived in the gaps between the plan.

We've all quietly agreed that the goal is to cut the inefficiency out of finding people. Front-load the information, reduce the randomness, maximize the match rate. It makes sense as a product. It doesn't make much sense as a life.

I'm not saying never. I know people who met on apps and are happy. I'm not claiming I'm doing better by staying off them. If I'm honest, I'm probably just doing lonelier.

But there's one corner I want to keep unoptimized. One thing left to chance and timing and the dumb luck of being somewhere, talking to someone, with no score attached.

The algorithm can have my music, my route, my feed, my Tuesday.

Not this.
