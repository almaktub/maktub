Do You Believe in Magic?

Published October 2, 2026

“But you hate tea.”

My mother said it like she was reminding me of my own name.

She had come home from my aunt’s house and walked into my room with what she called a random question. Did I remember how much green tea I used to drink when I was with my ex?

Of course I remembered. Thermoses of it, every day.

“And that didn’t seem strange to you?”

No. People pick up habits from the people they love. I figured tea was one of mine.

“I raised you,” she said. “You never drank tea.”

In an Afghan family, this is a known offense. My cousins had spent my entire childhood questioning my nationality because I drank water with everything. Tea could be poured all around me, and I would still ask for water. Then I got into a relationship and suddenly started drinking enough green tea that, years later, my mother was standing in my room asking me to account for it.

“Love is blind, Mom. Sometimes it makes you do stupid things.”

She agreed. Then she told me what she had heard.

My ex had gone to an Afghan mullah for something to put in my tea. My mother said she had confirmed it with the same man.

Afghan communities are tight enough that a private matter can take a long route and still arrive at your mother’s house. This particular mullah was known in Sacramento for spells and preparations involving tea leaves. Tea is already everywhere in our lives. Nobody has to persuade an Afghan household to put the kettle on.

Except, apparently, me.

I don’t know what was in those thermoses. I know what my mother told me, and I know that hearing it made me look again at years of my life I had never been able to explain.

Mostly, it made me think about B.

B is my cousin. She is also my sister. I need you to understand that before any of the rest makes sense.

I lost my older sister, G, years ago. That is another story, and I’m not ready to tell it here. Somewhere in the years that followed, B became a sister to me too. I couldn’t tell you when it happened. Eventually, calling her my cousin felt insufficient.

I met my ex through her. Before the relationship became serious, B warned me that we were fundamentally different, that I was walking into something that would hurt me. She tried to tell me in every way she could.

I heard her. I went ahead anyway.

As the relationship grew more serious, I grew more distant from B. My ex knew B distrusted her. I came to believe she wanted B out of my life, and over time, that was exactly where B ended up.

I missed birthdays, family gatherings, the birth of her daughter. My niece was born, and I wasn’t there.

The strangest part was that I couldn’t name what B had done to deserve any of it.

My mother had asked me before. Why aren’t you talking to her? What happened?

“It’s a long story, Mom.”

That answer worked because I never had to tell the story. I could resent the question, avoid the conversation, and keep going. Meanwhile, years passed for everyone else too. A child was born. People celebrated things. B lived a life I should have been part of.

By the night my mother asked about the tea, my ex and I had separated, and she had been dead for roughly three years. There was nobody left to confront. I had still not made things right with B.

My mother asked me again.

“I have time now. Tell me why you stopped talking to her.”

I tried.

Every explanation led back to my relationship. Things I had been told. Resentments I had absorbed. Feelings I had carried around as if they belonged entirely to me. I couldn’t find something B herself had done that justified shutting her out.

I had spent years saying the answer was too long. Sitting there with my mother, I couldn’t produce one.

I cried that night harder than I had in years, probably harder than I had since losing G. I thought about my niece being born. I thought about all the ordinary chances I’d had to pick up a phone.

Whatever I believed about the tea, I was the one who hadn’t called.

I told my mother I knew what I needed to do.

“I trust you,” she said.

The next day, I texted B. I told her I knew I had done wrong and that I understood if she didn’t want to speak to me. I asked if we could have a conversation.

She replied right away.

I was prepared for a list. She had years of material. If she wanted to walk me through every occasion I had disappointed her, I would have had to sit there and listen.

Instead, she told me that my recognizing what I had done gave her no reason to stay angry. Thank you for reaching out. We’re good.

That was it.

I had spent so long avoiding the conversation that I had imagined something much harder on the other side of it. She let me come back with an ease I still don’t quite know how to describe.

We started talking again. The familiarity was still there. So was everything I had missed.

Shortly afterward, a relative died in Northern California. He was my aunt’s father-in-law, though that doesn’t tell you much about who he was to me. We had lived together, and I thought of him as a grandfather. He used to tutor me in chemistry and Pashto. He was educated, generous, and part of my childhood.

Our mothers couldn’t make the journey for the burial that day. B and I agreed to go together, to represent them.

It was a practical arrangement. Somebody needed to be there. But seeing us go together after years apart meant something to our parents too. I hadn’t understood how much our estrangement had occupied other people’s lives until I saw what our reconciliation gave them.

I drove overnight. B had work that morning, so I had time before meeting her. Around four-thirty, I stopped in Monterey.

There’s a stretch of coast there I like to visit. I park above the ocean and usually have a cold Red Bull. That morning I didn’t have one, and I was low on gas. After sitting for a while, I drove to a gas station. There was a Starbucks across the street, just opening.

I ordered coffee and waited.

People were coming in with laptops and things to read. The staff were getting the place going. I was looking at my phone when a barista set a cup on the counter, then turned away before calling a name.

I reached to turn the cup and check whether it was mine.

A woman reached for it at the same time.

She had brown skin, long black hair, and several strings of beads. She picked up the cup.

“You don’t drink tea, remember? This is mine.”

I stopped.

“Excuse me?”

She took a small sip.

“You’re right,” I said. “I don’t drink tea. How do you know that?”

Maybe she had heard me order coffee. I considered that even then. But remember was a strange word to use with someone you had never met, especially a few days after his mother had stood in his room saying almost the same thing.

The woman smiled.

“I know a lot of things.”

Then she told me it was a long way to drive for a funeral.

“What funeral?”

“Come on. You know what funeral.”

I asked who she was. I asked how she knew.

The barista called my name, and I reached for my coffee while still looking at the woman.

She told me it had taken a lot to get me to this point. A lot of people’s prayers. She told me not to let it go to waste.

Then she said she didn’t know whether my mother could go through losing another child.

I froze.

I can offer myself an explanation for someone knowing I had ordered coffee. I can wonder whether I said something about the journey without remembering it. But my mother losing a child?

I had gone into a Starbucks to buy a drink. Now a stranger was talking about G.

I took my coffee. When I turned back, the woman was gone.

I know how that sounds. I have been trying to decide what to do with it ever since.

I believe the encounter happened as I remember it. I also know that telling you what happened doesn’t give me an explanation for it. I can tell you about the tea, what my mother said, and a woman in Monterey who seemed to know things I hadn’t told her. I can’t tell you who she was.

I walked outside and sat in my car. I texted B.

“Yo, you’re not going to fucking believe this.”

Then I called her.
