My Voice Is the Only Thing That's Actually Mine

June 18, 2026

My voice is the only thing that's actually mine.

Not my face — I got that from my father and my father's father before him, the same nose traveling down the line like a bad habit nobody has the sense to break. Not my taste in music — that was shaped by whatever cassette happened to be in the car on the day something clicked. Not even my opinions, really, which I've borrowed and stress-tested and rebuilt so many times I've lost track of which ones were originally mine and which ones I absorbed from a book I read at seventeen and never fully digested.

But the way I write. The rhythm of it. The specific place I choose to end a sentence. That, I built.

It took longer than I'd like to admit. I grew up speaking Farsi at home, Urdu with my grandparents, English everywhere else, and none of them particularly well for a long time. There was a period in my early teens where I existed in the gaps between all three — able to get my point across in any of them but not fully myself in any of them either. The Farsi I spoke was too simple for serious conversation. My Urdu had an accent that made my cousins smirk. And my English, for years, was this careful, assembled thing. I chose words the way you choose clothes for a job interview: functional, inoffensive, nothing that would get me laughed at.

Writing was worse. Reading my own sentences back then felt like watching someone do an impression of a person who reads a lot. The grammar was correct. The vocabulary was fine. It just didn't sound like a person. It sounded like someone trying very hard not to sound like they were trying.

At some point — I can't tell you exactly when — something shifted. The sentences started to have a shape I recognized. A rhythm that was mine and not borrowed. I'd write something and think: yes, that's actually what I meant to say, and it sounds like me saying it. That's a small thing to most people. To someone who spent years constructing a voice in a language that wasn't his first, second, or even third — it felt like something.

Then ChatGPT came out and everybody started using it to write.

I get it. I genuinely do. Writing is hard. Blank pages are hostile. Deadlines exist. And the tool is impressive in ways that are legitimately difficult to argue with. Ask it to write a product description, a cover letter, a birthday message for your coworker you've never spoken to — it handles all of it, instantly, competently. This is fine. I have no strong feelings about cover letters for coworkers you've never spoken to.

What I have feelings about is what happens when people start using it for the writing that's supposed to be theirs. The essays. The blog posts. The captions that are meant to tell you something about who a person actually is. There's a specific texture to AI-written prose that I can spot now the way you learn to spot cheap speakers in a high-end room — technically it's filling the space, but it's not coming from the right place. The sentences are structurally sound and completely lifeless. Every paragraph is organized like a persuasion attempt. The jokes land but they're the safest possible version of the joke. The insight is present but it's been sanded down until there's nothing sharp left to cut you.

And people are reading it. Publishing it. Putting their name on it.

Here is what I think is actually happening: a lot of people never had the experience of developing a voice in the first place. Not because they're not smart or interesting, but because nobody ever told them that the way they naturally wrote was worth keeping. School rewarded the formal register. The internet rewarded the punchy hook. They spent years flattening themselves into whichever shape the context demanded, and then a tool arrived that does the flattening for them and does it faster, and that felt like help.

I understand this. I lived a version of it. The difference is that I had to fight my way to a voice the hard way, in a language I didn't grow up in, and that fight left a mark I can actually hear when I read what I write. It sounds like a person who had to assemble themselves from parts that didn't quite fit. It sounds like someone whose brain runs in three languages simultaneously and occasionally leaves pieces of one sitting in the middle of another. It sounds like me, which is the only thing I was ever actually trying to achieve.

When I hand that to an AI and ask it to "improve" this, what comes back is cleaner and worse. The grammar is tighter. The structure is more logical. Every sentence that had a slight lean to it — the kind of lean that comes from thinking in Farsi and writing in English — has been straightened out. What's left is correct in every technical sense and it belongs to no one. It could have been written by anyone. It was, in a way.

I don't think AI is going to kill writing. But I do think it's going to kill voices. Not all at once. Slowly, the way anything gets lost — one small compromise at a time, each one reasonable on its own, until you look up one day and can't remember what the original sounded like.

The people who will resist this are the ones who know, on some cellular level, that their voice cost them something to build. You don't give away for free what you paid for with years of sounding wrong in public.

Everything else about me is borrowed. The voice is mine. I'm keeping it.
