He Just Needs a Wife

Published June 9, 2026

Back home, they had a diagnosis for everything that wasn't a broken bone.

Get him married.

That was the treatment plan. Man in his twenties acting strange, withdrawn, quiet, angry for no reason? Get him married. Something heavy sitting on his chest he can't explain? Get him married. Can't sleep, can't eat, can't tell you why? Brother, that's a wife problem. Solve that and you solve the rest.

And if he was already married? Then it was a kid problem. Have a kid. That'll give you purpose. That'll fill whatever hollow thing you're carrying around and pretending isn't there.

Nobody asked what the hollow thing was. You don't go looking at a wound like that. You cover it. You marry it off. You pass it down to the next generation dressed up as a solution, and they'll figure it out or they won't, but at least now it's their problem.

It wasn't cruel. It was resourceful, in a way that made new problems as fast as it solved the old ones.

I didn't know the word depression until I was well into my twenties. I didn't know anxiety was a clinical thing and not just being nervous. I didn't know the noise in my head, the constant opinionated noise that never shut up, had a name. A category. A treatment protocol. I thought that was just what being a person felt like.

Back home, we called the real cases crazy. The ones who couldn't hide it anymore, whose lid finally came off. Everything short of that was just life. Just what you carried.

Then I moved, and I watched people talk about therapy like a dentist appointment. Casual. Routine. People said "I'm in a depressive episode" the way I'd say "I have a cold." There was language for it. There were professionals. There were, eventually, pills.

The medication doesn't fix anything.

You wake up the same. Most mornings you still don't like yourself very much. The things that bother you still bother you. The thoughts still show up uninvited, like relatives who don't call ahead.

But the volume is lower.

You still hear everything, but now you have a knob. You can't turn it off. You can turn it down. The day I realized I could actually manage the level of my own internal noise, that I could step in on myself, felt like finding a room in my own house that had always been there and that I'd never once opened.

Then the uncomfortable part.

Two days without the meds and I'm losing it. Not dramatically. Nothing you'd notice from the outside. But the volume goes back up. The thoughts get louder. The version of me I worked to quiet comes back like it never agreed to leave.

Which leaves me with a question: did the West give me a condition, or give me language for something I was already living with?

I can't tell. Maybe the diagnosis was always true and I just didn't have the word. Maybe I was the cousin someone pointed at and said "he needs a wife," and everyone nodded and moved on.

Or maybe I learned it. Picked up the vocabulary, the framework, and my brain, being the cooperative organ it is, started performing it back.

Back home they had a solution. It was wrong. It was inherited. It cost people things they never got back. But it was clean. You didn't have to sit with the question. You got married and hoped for the best.

Now I have a name for it. A pill for it. Two days off it and I can feel exactly what it does and doesn't do for me.

The problem with learning to hear yourself is that you can't unhear what you find.
