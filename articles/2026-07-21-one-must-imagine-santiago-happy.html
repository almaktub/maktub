One Must Imagine Santiago Happy

July 21, 2026

I bought The Old Man and the Sea, Hemingway's final novella, without knowing a thing about it.

It joined the Everest of books I really must get around to reading sometime, a stack that had quietly reached record-breaking heights over the past year. It had been the busiest, most stressful stretch of my professional life. I barely found time to sleep, let alone read. (One can always find time to buy books, of course.)

It also tipped me headfirst into burnout, the kind I couldn't escape while the deadlines kept coming. I missed each one with admirable consistency, always finding some last little bit left in the tank to get me over the line. That lasted until January, when the tank (or rather, my body) finally gave out under the weight of it.

Burnout had razed me to the ground. I was catatonic. "Time to read," I thought.

Somewhere in that stretch of doing nothing but sleeping and reading, I opened The Old Man and the Sea.

It tells a story of hope and human resilience in the face of adversity. Or at least, it tries to. I arrived at its pages still semi-catatonic and, critically, having read Albert Camus' The Myth of Sisyphus just hours before (an arguably dangerous combination for someone already in crisis).

The actual myth of Sisyphus, before Camus got his existentially angsty hands on it, is an ancient Greek tale about a mortal king who made the unfortunate mistake of ruffling godly feathers. As punishment, he was condemned to roll a boulder uphill for all eternity. Over time, poor Sisyphus has been co-opted as shorthand for laborious, unending tasks. Quite the fall from grace.

Camus' The Myth of Sisyphus, in contrast, is at its core a reflection on whether life is worth living. It famously ends with the line: "One must imagine Sisyphus happy." Why? Because there is no alternative. Life is absurd, hope a delusion, and the boulder isn't going anywhere.

Camus prescribes rebellion. Once you accept life's meaninglessness, he argues, you have two choices: collapse under the weight of existential dread, or keep pushing the rock anyway. Not because you expect it to resolve, but because the pushing itself has to be enough. Find some grim satisfaction in that, and congratulations, you win. Sisyphus becomes the absurd hero. Way to turn it around, Sisyphus.

"The struggle itself towards the heights is enough to fill a man's heart," Camus assures us. It's not exactly comforting.

And there I was, existentially tender and freshly Camus-rattled, watching the old man hook his marlin and fight it for three days. When he finally dragged its ravaged carcass back to shore, stripped clean by sharks, what did he have to show for it? Nothing. It felt Sisyphean. Absurd.

I flipped to the back of the book looking for some kind of buffer. An epilogue, a postscript, a footnote clarifying that the real prize was the friends he made along the way. Instead I found the Also by the Author page, laid out with all the warmth of a coroner's report.

And then the last line: "Hemingway committed suicide in 1961." I stopped breathing. "Fuuuuuuuuuck."

1961. Which meant The Old Man and the Sea, this story about resilience, about not giving up, was written just nine years earlier. It had won him the Nobel Prize he'd always wanted.

He would have been around sixty when he chose to die. About the same age my mother was when she did.

Suicide rates, statistically speaking, tend to be highest in older age. Which feels counterintuitive. You're almost there, right? You've fought your battles, raised your kids, made it through. Why quit so close to the finish line?

But the older you get, the more loss compounds, and the more your sense of possibility shrinks. Paths that were once open seal off behind you. The list of things that could happen narrows down to the one life you actually did. And if that life, when you take stock of it, feels like a series of fights won only to be undone, if it feels Sisyphean, then what's left?

One thing Camus and I agree on, among many things, is that hope, religion, and the search for inherent meaning make us fragile. They offer comfort, sure, but only as long as it holds up. All it takes is one good kick (grief, failure, some blunt form of entropy) and those defenses collapse. You're left standing in the rubble, staring straight into the absurd. Cold. Brutal. Indifferent. This is when people choose to die.

The longer you're alive, the greater the odds of hitting that moment. The absurd will find you. And when it does, it will ask: was it all worth it? Knowing what you know now, will you still push the boulder? Hemingway, it seems, answered no.

And yet, whether he meant to or not, he gave us one of the clearest illustrations of absurdism in fiction.

Santiago, for all his suffering, treats the marlin with care. He talks to it. He respects it. He calls it his brother. When he finally kills it, he does so with reverence, not victory. And when the sharks come, carving away the prize he fought so hard to bring home, he doesn't curse the universe or shake his fist at the sky. He simply fights, even knowing the fight is futile.

It holds the piece The Myth of Sisyphus doesn't quite say out loud. Life is cruel, the universe indifferent. But we are not. We see beauty. We feel it. And we care for it. Santiago suffers, but he never becomes cruel. He never takes on the indifference of the universe.

That, I think, is the better answer to the absurd. Not Camus' cold assurance that "the struggle is enough to fill a man's heart." Not "enjoy the grind, suckers." Something softer: enjoy the scenery.

We, who were never meant to be here, who were spat into a world with no inherent meaning, went ahead and made some anyway. Not just through rebellion, not simply by pushing the rock, but by stopping, when we can, to admire the scenery. By letting ourselves be delighted anyway, by a book that turns out to say the thing you needed to hear the week you needed to hear it.

Absurdism is nihilism warmed up. It steadies your resolve without hardening your heart. Nihilism tends to make people insufferable, the kind of bitter cynic you regret inviting to your dinner table. Absurdism lets you be delighted, lets you make meaning for yourself, lets you behave like an optimist despite knowing better.

And it lets you, like Santiago, treat life with more tenderness than it has ever shown you.

When I explain absurdism to the uninitiated, I borrow a Bill Hicks line: life is just a ride. There's freedom in that. It's what lets you look suffering in the face and, instead of collapsing, laugh. Because what else can you do? Life is just a ride. And unlike Sisyphus, we're not condemned to it forever. The wheels stop turning eventually. The track ends.

Might as well enjoy the scenery while it runs.
