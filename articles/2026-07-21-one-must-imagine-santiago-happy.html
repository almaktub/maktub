One Must Imagine Santiago Happy

July 21, 2026

The paperback sat in the door pocket of my work truck for about four months before I opened it.

That's where my reading happens now, and never in one sitting. Twenty minutes here, a few pages there, engine running for the air conditioning, somebody's driveway, somebody's sprinklers coming on at the same time every afternoon. It took me the better part of a month to get through ninety-odd pages that way, which sounds pathetic until you remember the book is about a man doing one thing slowly for three days. I'd bought it because it was short and because Hemingway is one of those names you're supposed to have dealt with by a certain age. Then I let it sit there through spring. Buying books and reading books are two separate hobbies that happen to use the same equipment, and I've always been better at the first one.

I already knew how he died. Most people do. It arrives attached to the name whether you went looking for it or not, the way a few facts about a few famous people just get issued to you. So I never got the version of this book that ends in an ambush, where you close the last page feeling lifted and then find out later. I had it going in, which turned out to be its own kind of problem.

Here's the book. An old Cuban fisherman hasn't caught anything in eighty-four days. The boy who used to work his boat has been moved to a luckier one by his parents. So the old man goes out further than sense allows, alone, and hooks a marlin longer than his skiff. He fights it for three days. He wins. And on the way back the sharks find the blood trail and take the fish apart in pieces, and he ties up at his village with a spine and a tail lashed to the side of a boat, and goes to sleep.

Nine years after that book came out, and seven after it finally got him the Nobel he'd wanted his whole life, Hemingway ended his own life. He was sixty-one.

I'd read Camus a few years before any of this, and I've been arguing with him ever since, which I think is the correct relationship to have with Camus.

The original Sisyphus wasn't a martyr. He was a king who kept outsmarting death, literally, chaining up the god sent to collect him and then talking his way back out of the underworld after he'd already been delivered. The gods finally settled it by handing him a rock and a hill and no expiration date. Push it up, watch it roll down, do it again. That's the whole sentence. Not fire, not torment, just work with nothing at the end of it. His name has since been reduced to an adjective for bad jobs, which is a thin retirement for a man whose real offense was being too clever about dying.

Camus picks the story up and uses it to ask the one question he thinks philosophy has any business asking, which is whether a person should go on living at all. His answer is yes, but not for any of the usual reasons. There's no meaning stitched into the universe waiting to be found. There's no ledger. Hope, in his reading, is a form of borrowing against a future that never signed anything. What's left is to keep pushing with your eyes open, knowing the rock comes back down, and to locate something inside the pushing itself. "The struggle itself towards the heights is enough to fill a man's heart," he says. Then the last line of the essay, the one everybody knows: one must imagine Sisyphus happy.

I've never been able to accept that as comfort. Telling a man the hill is permanent and then instructing him to feel good about his shoulders is not the encouragement it dresses itself up as. It reads to me like a very intelligent person talking himself off a ledge and then publishing the transcript as advice.

Which is what I was carrying into the driveway, and why the marlin coming home as a skeleton felt less like tragedy and more like confirmation.

Except that's not what the book actually does, and I missed it the first time through.

Santiago knows he's losing. Not at the end, either. He works it out fairly early, somewhere in the second day, and he keeps going anyway, and the part that matters is how he behaves while he does it. He talks to the fish. He calls it his brother. He says out loud that he's sorry it has to go this way and there is nobody in that boat to hear him say it, no audience, no credit. When he finally kills it he does it with something closer to apology than triumph. Then the sharks come and he fights them with a knife lashed to an oar until the knife snaps, and he loses, and he still doesn't curse the water or the sky or his own luck. He just goes home and sleeps and dreams about lions on a beach.

That's the thing Camus leaves out. The universe is indifferent, agreed, no argument, the rock isn't going anywhere. But Santiago isn't indifferent, and he refuses to become it. He takes the cruelty of the arrangement and declines to pass it on.

I run cable for a living. Almost everything I do vanishes into a wall the same afternoon I do it. Somebody buys that house in fifteen years, guts it, and my work goes into a dumpster without anyone ever knowing whether it was good. If you're shopping for a boulder, there's one. There have been whole weeks where the job felt like a sentence rather than a trade.

But the days I actually like are the ones where I take the extra twenty minutes on a run nobody will ever see. Dress it properly. Label both ends. Not for the client, who will never open that wall, and not for my boss, who isn't there. For no reason available to anyone but me. That isn't resilience and it isn't rebellion either. It's closer to manners. You treat the work decently because being the kind of man who doesn't is a worse outcome than the work being pointless.

So I'll take Santiago over Sisyphus. Enduring is the smaller achievement, and anybody desperate enough will manage it. Staying kind while you lose is the one that costs something, because there's no reward attached and nobody checking.

Hemingway wrote a man who could do that, and then couldn't do it himself. I don't read that as hypocrisy and I don't think the book is a lie he told. I think he knew exactly where the answer was and couldn't get to it from where he was standing, which is a sadder and more ordinary thing. People tend to write the thing they need. Sometimes it doesn't reach them in time.

The rock was never really the test. Anyone can push. What the world asks, quietly, in the flattest voice it owns, on a Tuesday, is whether you're going to turn into it.

Santiago said no with a broken knife and nothing to show for it. That's the answer I want. Not that the struggle was enough.

That I didn't get cruel on the way down.
