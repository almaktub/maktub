Neither Here Nor There

Or, The Permanent Art of Being the Wrong Kind of Person Everywhere
June 1, 2026 · 12:32 AM

There is a very specific personality disorder that is not in the DSM but absolutely should be.

It is called: being an immigrant kid.

Or worse, being the child of immigrants. I'm not just living one life. I'm running two operating systems at once and both of them keep asking for updates.

I'm too foreign at home. Too local abroad. Too Western for the cousins, too ethnic for the coworkers. Too much of one thing for one room, not enough for the next.

I've spent my whole life feeling like a software glitch in human form.

Congratulations, I'm the bridge. Sounds poetic until you remember bridges just get walked on.

This starts early. As a kid I figured out fast that my house wasn't like other people's houses. At school my lunch smelled like culture. At home, anything that came out of a wrapper instead of a pot was treated like a personal insult to the family.

Other kids had names teachers could pronounce. I had a name substitute teachers approached like they were diffusing a bomb. They'd stare at the attendance sheet like my existence was a pronunciation challenge.

"Is… uh… Say-ed? Syed? Sayed? Samuel?"

Sure. Let's go with Kevin.

I learned to laugh. Because if I didn't, I'd have to explain my entire family tree before first period.

Then there were the family parties. The Olympics of identity confusion. I'd walk in and get hit with three things at once: food, interrogation, and an aunt I'd never seen before asking why I'd gotten so skinny and so fat at the same time.

Relatives would speak to me in my mother tongue with absolute confidence. I'd reply like Google Translate during a power outage. Half a sentence, three English words, a nervous smile. They'd look at me like my ancestors were filing formal complaints.

"Your language is getting weak."

Yes, because apparently I chose to be born in subtitles.

At home, they'd say: "Why are you acting so American?" Outside, people ask: "Where are you really from?"

Nowhere. Everywhere. Spiritually? The airport.

That question is one of history's great passive-aggressive magic tricks. It sounds innocent. What it actually means is: I seem like I belong here, suspiciously, please explain.

And if I answered with my actual hometown? "No, but originally?"

Brother, I was three years old. My original form was crying. What answer are we looking for?

Sometimes I want to say: I'm from the deep emotional damage section. Aisle seven. Near the generational trauma.

Here's the part I haven't actually tested. I haven't been back since we left. Not once. And the closer that trip gets to being a real possibility instead of a someday thing, the more one specific fear shows up: that I'd walk in thinking finally, my people, and get clocked in about four seconds flat. The accent. The clothes. Some tell I can't even predict yet, the way I'd probably ask for the Wi-Fi password before I asked for water. That I'd go looking for my roots and find out my roots think I'm weird.

I don't know any of that is true. I've just turned it over enough times that it stopped feeling hypothetical. It's the specific kind of homesick where you're afraid of the place agreeing with you that you don't belong there anymore either.

Even language betrays me. I think in one language, feel in another, argue in English, apologize in my mother tongue, panic in both.

Some emotions don't even translate. There are words my parents use that take five English sentences and still lose their emotional violence. Especially disappointment. Immigrant-parent disappointment is an art form.

No one in my family says "I'm disappointed" like a dramatic American movie parent. They say "Do whatever you want," and suddenly I feel like I've personally ruined civilization. That sentence has the emotional power of a court summons.

And let's talk about careers. Immigrant households don't believe in "following your passion." Passion is for people with trust funds. My options were doctor, engineer, lawyer, or family disappointment. Maybe pharmacist if my aunt was feeling progressive.

I'd say, "I want to write." They'd hear "I would like to be unemployed professionally."

I'd say "I'm exploring." They'd hear "I have declared war on stability."

I could cure loneliness itself and someone would still ask, "Yes, but is there dental insurance?"

Honestly, fair. These people crossed oceans. They didn't survive dictatorship, war, and paperwork for me to become a podcast host. Be serious.

And then there's romance. Diaspora dating deserves its own United Nations committee. I'm trying to find love while balancing my own standards, my parents' standards, my religion's standards, my aunt's standards for some reason, and a cousin who keeps sending biodata like she works for LinkedIn Marriage Edition.

I bring someone home and suddenly everyone becomes an FBI profiler. "What does their father do?" I don't know, man, I just like how they laugh. Apparently that's not enough. Love is beautiful. But can love speak the language and own property? Separate questions, apparently.

Beneath all the comedy there's something heavier. Living between worlds means constantly editing myself. At work I soften my name. At home I sharpen my accent. With friends I explain my parents. With my parents I explain my friends. I've become a translator for everyone, including myself.

After enough years I started wondering which version was actually me. The one at home? The one outside? The one who code-switches so fast it deserves an Olympic medal? Or is identity just motion?

Maybe being "in-between" isn't confusion. Maybe it's fluency. Maybe belonging to two worlds means I get to see both more clearly. Maybe being neither is secretly being both.

It took me a while to learn that. For years I thought home was a place I had to choose. This country or that one. This language or that one. This version of myself or the other.

But maybe home isn't geography. Maybe home is recognition. The people who understand my joke before I finish it. The smell of food that fixes my mood. The song my parents played while cleaning on Sundays. The friend who also lies to their family by saying they're "five minutes away."

Home is where explanation becomes unnecessary.

And sometimes, weirdly, home is the tension itself. The in-between. The airport gate. The phone call. The half-translated sentence. The place where contradiction lives.

Identity isn't always a clean answer. Sometimes it's just learning to stop apologizing for being complicated.

I'm allowed to belong in more than one place. I'm allowed to be made of mixed things: language, memory, migration, loss, humor, shame, pride. I'm allowed to be unfinished. That's not failure. That's inheritance.

So yes, diaspora life is funny. It's aunties judging my life choices while handing me dessert. It's explaining to friends why "take food home" isn't a suggestion, it's a legal requirement. It's being told to "act normal" by people who believe turmeric can fix a Wi-Fi problem.

It's chaos. It's also beautiful. There's something powerful about being proof that survival traveled, that my family crossed oceans and I'm here, bad at my native language, good at pretending I understand taxes, emotionally attached to both countries during the World Cup.

That matters. I'm not lost. I'm evidence.

And maybe that is enough.

So if someone asks where I'm really from, now I just smile and say: depends. Emotionally or geographically?

Because geographically, I can explain. Emotionally? Honestly, I'm from a kitchen where nobody said "I love you," but everyone asked if I wanted more rice.

And if that doesn't make sense to them, that's okay. Some people need Google Maps. Some of us were raised by directions like: "Turn where the old bakery used to be."

And we still found our way home.
