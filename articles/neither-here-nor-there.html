Neither Here Nor There

Or, The Permanent Art of Being the Wrong Kind of Person Everywhere
Published January 24, 2021

I was born in Pakistan to Afghan parents. I started school in Urdu, and that was home. Then the Taliban fell, every Afghan family I knew decided it was time to go back, and at eight I was in Kabul relearning multiplication tables I already knew cold, this time in Dari, as the kid who talked wrong. At twenty I landed in San Diego and started over again in English.

Three countries. In every one of them, sooner or later, someone asked me where I was really from.

Farsi at home, Urdu with my grandparents, English everywhere else. I think in one, feel in another, argue in English, apologize in Dari, and panic in all three at once. None of them have ever fully felt like mine. The relatives back home will tell you, kindly and constantly, that my language is getting weak. Yes. Apparently I chose to be born in subtitles.

Then there's the name. Every first day of class in this country, the same ritual. The teacher gets to my name on the roster and stops like they're defusing a bomb.

"Is it... uh... Say-ed? Syed? Samuel?"

Sure. Let's go with Kevin.

I learned to laugh, because the alternative was explaining my entire family tree before the lecture started.

Family gatherings are their own weather system. I walk in and the reviews start before I've taken my shoes off. One aunt tells me I've gotten fat. An uncle across the room tells me I've gotten so skinny. Same night. Same me. Both of them completely sure.

That's what living between places is like, in one evening. Everyone has a clear picture of you, and none of the pictures agree.

At home I was too American. Out here, people ask where I'm really from. If I give them the honest answer, Pakistan, then Afghanistan, then here, they look at me like I've given them a riddle. If I say Afghanistan, they ask "no, but originally?"

Nowhere. Everywhere. Spiritually? The airport.

The part I haven't tested yet is going back. I haven't been to Afghanistan since we left in 2014. Not once. And the closer that trip gets to being real instead of a someday thing, the more one specific fear shows up. I'd walk in thinking finally, my people, and get clocked in about four seconds. The accent. The clothes. Some tell I can't even predict yet, like asking for the Wi-Fi password before asking for water. I'd go looking for my roots and find out my roots think I'm weird.

I don't know if any of that would happen. I've just turned it over so many times it stopped feeling hypothetical. It's a specific kind of homesick: being afraid the place will agree with you that you don't belong there anymore.

For a long time I thought the answer was to pick one. This country or that one. This language or that one. This version of me or the other. Every move made me shave off a little more of whatever the last place had built, so I'd fit the next one faster.

I've stopped trying. What I've got isn't one place, it's a short list of people who get the joke before I finish it. My brothers yelling my name across the house. My cousin B. My mother, who still won't give me a recipe.

So when somebody asks where I'm really from, I give them the short answer now and keep the long one for the people who don't need it.

The long one starts in an airport.
