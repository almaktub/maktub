Painkiller Smile

Published September 17, 2026

On the drive home, I was explaining a Joy Crookes song to my phone when I started talking about my ex.

This was supposed to be a close reading. Rihanna references, wordplay, a little detective work. I had checked Genius to see whether anyone had caught what I was hearing, and finding nothing there pleased me more than I’d like to admit. For a few minutes, the interpretations were mine. Possibly wrong, but mine.

I get like this with songs. A phrase catches my attention and suddenly I need to know what state of mind someone was in when they wrote it. What they noticed. Whether they spent three days looking for a word or said it by accident and had the sense to keep it. Sometimes I’m so busy following a line somewhere that I miss the next three.

Knowing more usually makes me feel more. A reference gives me another reason to return to a verse. A line I didn’t understand becomes the part I wait for. I like hearing something familiar and realizing there was still something in it I hadn’t caught.

There’s a little pride involved too. Otherwise, I wouldn’t have spent part of a voice note assuring an audience of nobody that I hadn’t borrowed my interpretation from Genius. I wanted to know whether I was right, but I also liked arriving before anyone had put up an explanation. I could sit with what I thought for a while.

Also, I have a ridiculous crush on Joy Crookes. This feels relevant to the integrity of the criticism.

I first found her through FIFA. Now I was driving home, working my way through “Painkiller,” stopping to admire a line, then talking over the next one. At one point, I accidentally rhymed while explaining her wordplay and interrupted myself to appreciate my own contribution.

Then she sang “painkiller smile,” and I knew exactly whose face I was looking at.

I used to tell my ex she had a smile that could melt a mountain. Those were my actual words. I said them to her, probably with the complete confidence of a man who thought he had invented mountains.

There was an innocence to her smile that I loved. Even now, knowing everything else, I can remember it clearly. That’s one of the things I still don’t quite know what to do with. I can spend so much time questioning the relationship, and that part comes back without any effort at all.

We were long distance. A lot of our relationship happened over FaceTime, with visits that eventually returned us to an airport. At the end of a visit, she would tell me to text when I got home, or have a safe flight, depending on who was leaving. She’d tell me she loved me.

I would ask her for something.

“Smile for me one time, my love.”

I wanted the world to brighten up a bit before we had to go back to looking at each other through a screen.

I meant that. Whatever happened later hasn’t made that part untrue. I loved her smile when we were happy. I asked for it because we were leaving each other and I wanted to see it once more. I don’t want to go back and turn every tender thing between us into evidence of something I should have known.

But there were other times when she hurt my feelings or crossed a boundary, and the same smile could make everything feel okay again.

That’s harder to remember honestly. I can see the pattern now, which makes it tempting to describe every instance as though I understood it then. I didn’t. Sometimes I believed we were okay. Sometimes I think I wanted to stop feeling hurt badly enough that I accepted the first thing that helped.

I’m still sorting out which was which.

I can understand why I wanted things to be okay with someone I loved. What troubles me is how often that feeling seemed to settle the matter. Something had hurt me, then she smiled, and I softened. The thing I needed to say became easier to let go.

After the way it ended, I went back over the relationship, questioning little details, finding things I felt I should have understood sooner. There’s a particular frustration in remembering a moment and wanting to lend your past self everything you know now. I keep thinking I could have said something more firmly. Held a boundary. Stayed with the uncomfortable conversation a little longer.

From here, I can point to moments when I wish I had acted differently. Remembering how I felt inside those moments is more difficult. I have to remember that I loved her, that her smile could still reach me, that I hadn’t yet lived through the ending I now use to explain everything before it.

I’m wary of saying the hurt made me better. I can say I recognize things I used to let pass. I can also say I still question myself. Some days, understanding the pattern just gives me more questions about why I stayed in it for so long.

That’s what I heard in those two words on the drive home. The relief was real. Her smile worked. What I’m only beginning to understand is how often I took feeling better as a reason to leave the original hurt alone.

I don’t know whether that’s everything Joy meant. I had already spent part of the drive confidently explaining references I might have misunderstood. Usually, that would send me looking for an annotation, an interview, someone who could tell me whether I had caught the meaning.

This time, I knew why I kept returning to the chorus.

It brought me back to the airport, asking the woman I loved to smile for me.

I still understand why I asked.
