Painkiller Smile

Published September 17, 2026

On the drive home, I was lost in lyrics to a song by Joy Crookes that reminded me of my ex.

This was supposed to be a close reading. Rihanna references, wordplay, a little detective work. I'd checked Genius first to see if anyone had caught what I was hearing, and finding nothing there made me happier than I'd like to admit. For a few minutes the reading was mine. Possibly wrong, but mine.

I get like this with songs. A phrase grabs me and suddenly I need to know what state someone was in when they wrote it. Whether they spent three days hunting for a word or said it by accident and had the sense to keep it. I get so busy following one line somewhere that I miss the next three.

There's some pride in it too. Otherwise I wouldn't have spent part of a voice note assuring an audience of nobody that I hadn't borrowed my take from Genius.

Also, I have a ridiculous crush on Joy Crookes. This feels relevant to the integrity of the criticism.

I found her through FIFA. "Feet Don't Fail Me Now" was on the soundtrack, and that was it for me. Now I was driving home working through "Painkiller," stopping to admire a line, then talking over the next one. At one point I accidentally rhymed while explaining her wordplay and stopped to appreciate my own contribution.

Then she sang "painkiller smile," and I knew exactly whose face I was looking at.

I used to tell my ex she had a smile that could melt a mountain. Those were my actual words. I said them to her face, with the full confidence of a man who thought he'd invented mountains.

There was an innocence in that smile I loved. Even now, knowing everything else, it comes back clean. I can spend a whole drive picking the relationship apart and that part shows up untouched.

We were long distance. Most of it happened over FaceTime, with visits that always ended at an airport, Burbank or San Diego. Whoever was leaving, she'd say text me when you land, or have a safe flight. She'd say she loved me.

And I'd ask for one thing.

"Smile for me one time, my love."

I wanted the world a little brighter before we went back to looking at each other through a screen.

I meant it. Nothing that happened later makes that untrue, and I'm not going back to turn every tender thing between us into evidence.

But there were other times. She'd hurt me, or cross a line, and the same smile would make it all feel fine again. It happened far more often than I let myself notice.

Something hurt. She smiled. I softened. Whatever I'd needed to say got easier to drop.

After it ended I went back over all of it, the way you do, wanting to reach into the past and hand my old self everything I know now. Say it firmer. Hold the line. Stay in the uncomfortable conversation five more minutes. But inside those moments I loved her, the smile still reached me, and I hadn't yet lived through the ending I now use to explain everything that came before it.

That's what I heard in those two words on the drive. The relief was real. Her smile worked. What I'm only starting to see is how often I took feeling better as permission to leave the hurt where it was.

I don't know if that's what Joy meant. I'd spent half the drive confidently explaining references I might have gotten wrong. Usually that sends me hunting for an interview or an annotation, somebody to tell me whether I got it right.

This time I didn't need anyone to check my reading.

It took me back to the airport, asking the woman I loved to smile for me.

I still understand why I asked.
