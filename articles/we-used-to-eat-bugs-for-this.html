We Used to Eat Bugs For This

Published July 3, 2024

There was a time when reality television had standards.

Low ones, sure. But standards.

I'm talking about the era when a man would lower himself into a tank of cockroaches for twenty seconds of airtime and a cash prize that, after taxes, barely covered rent. When people ate sheep eyeballs on national television and we watched through our fingers, unsure whether to laugh or call someone. When the whole point was to humiliate yourself creatively: an obstacle course, a wilderness survival challenge, a room full of strangers voting you off an island because you were bad at making fire.

That was reality TV. Stupid, chaotic, occasionally cruel, but honest about what it was. A circus. And we showed up for the circus.

Now we watch people fall in love in a villa in Bali and immediately cheat on each other with someone who just walked in wearing a bikini. We watch housewives from various American zip codes throw wine glasses across a dinner table that cost more than my first car. We watch grown adults cry in confessional booths about whether someone is "here for the right reasons," a phrase that has survived fifteen seasons of a show built entirely on the wrong ones.

And we can't stop watching.

I'm not above this. I have sat on a couch, fully conscious, a functional adult with opinions about literature and a working understanding of Camus, and watched forty-five minutes of people arguing about who said what to whom in a hot tub. Voluntarily. I have, on occasion, watched the next episode immediately after.

So this isn't a takedown from someone standing outside the building. I am in the building. I am in the building eating snacks.

Still, something shifted. Reality television used to be about doing things: surviving, competing, performing, eating the sheep eyeball. The drama was a side effect. Now the drama is the product. Nobody's surviving anything, learning a skill, racing around the world, or getting dropped in a forest with a knife and some hubris. They're just existing near each other, and apparently that's enough.

You could argue, and people do, that this is what audiences wanted all along. The obstacle courses were packaging, and the real draw was always the human mess underneath. The alliances. The betrayals. The person who seemed normal in episode one and turned out to be an absolute menace by episode four.

Maybe. Maybe we always wanted the mess and the producers finally stopped pretending otherwise.

But I think something else happened too. Somewhere along the way the shows stopped being about watching other people be messy and started being about us feeling something, anything, in a life that keeps asking us to go numb. The outrage at the cheating. The secondhand embarrassment of the cringe confession. The real emotional investment in whether two people who met eleven days ago, with the combined emotional intelligence of a parking ticket, end up together.

It's feeling by proxy, and we're hooked.

The flatter real life gets, the commute, the inbox, the group chat that's been muted for six months, the more we need someone, somewhere, to be throwing a wine glass. Not at us. Just near us. Just so we remember what temperature feels like.

We all know how these shows work by now. The villain was cast on purpose. The "unexpected twist" was in the contract. A producer off camera is asking leading questions to start a fight between two people who were fine twenty minutes ago. We know all of it.

And we watch anyway.

So we're not being fooled. We're choosing this, over and over, with full information.

That used to bother me. Now I think it's at least honest. Reality TV doesn't pretend to be good for you. It's junk food that knows it's junk food, in a world full of content that wants you to feel enriched while it quietly takes everything.

The real question isn't why we watch Love Island.

It's why we watch Love Island instead of calling the person we've been meaning to call for three months, having the conversation we keep avoiding, or sitting alone with our thoughts for twenty minutes without reaching for a screen.

But that's a harder show to pitch.

No villa. No bikinis. No dramatic music sting when someone walks in from the other side of the room.

Just you, a quiet room, and the mild horror of your own interiority.

Zero viewers. Cancelled after one episode.
